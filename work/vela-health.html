A clinician dashboard that triages 3.1x faster

Vela Health's care teams were missing high-risk patients in a noisy, chronological alert feed. I redesigned the triage experience around clinical severity — without touching their underlying data model.

Client
Vela Health (remote care)
Role
Senior Product Designer
Timeline
14 weeks
Team
1 PM · 3 engineers · me
Overview The problem Constraints Process Key decisions Outcomes Reflection

Overview

Scaling caseload without scaling headcount.

Vela Health runs remote chronic-care programs for patients discharged from hospital care. Their clinical team monitors continuous vitals and patient-reported data through a dashboard — but as their patient base grew, clinicians were increasingly missing early warning signs buried in an undifferentiated, chronological alert feed. Growth was being blocked by UX, not by clinical capacity.

The problem

A feed that treated every alert as equally urgent.

The legacy dashboard listed patient alerts in the order they arrived — a mildly elevated heart-rate reading sat visually identical to a dangerous blood-oxygen drop. Clinicians developed manual workarounds (personal spreadsheets, sticky notes) to track what actually needed attention, which didn't scale and introduced real clinical risk. Shadowing sessions surfaced a recurring pattern: alert fatigue was causing genuinely high-risk cases to be triaged later than they should have been.

Constraints

Designing inside a live clinical system.

Compliance

All patient data handling had to remain HIPAA-compliant; no new data collection or third-party tooling could be introduced mid-project.

Data model

The backend severity-scoring logic couldn't change in this cycle — the redesign had to work with existing data, not new fields.

Dual audiences

Nurse-practitioners and physicians used the dashboard differently — one needed rapid triage, the other deeper case review — and both had to be served by one system.

Design debt

The existing design system had accumulated inconsistent patterns across three years of fast shipping; I could extend it, not rebuild it wholesale, given the timeline.

Process

Design research, done in scrubs.

  • Shadowed 8 clinicians during live triage sessions to see exactly how they scanned, prioritized, and acted on alerts in real time.
  • Built a jobs-to-be-done map distinguishing "rapid triage" moments from "deep case review" moments — they needed different interfaces, not one compromise.
  • Ran card sorting with clinical staff to validate the information hierarchy before any interface work began.
  • Designed a severity-first triage queue and tested it across three rounds with working clinicians, not proxies.
  • Extended the existing design system with new severity and status patterns, documented for the two other clinical modules already on the roadmap.

Key decisions

Severity first, chronology second.

Severity + recency scored queue

Replaced the chronological feed with a triage queue ranked by clinical severity first, recency second — surfacing the cases that needed attention now, not just the cases that arrived last.

At-a-glance risk cards

Designed compact patient cards that surfaced the two or three data points that actually drove a triage decision, instead of requiring a click into a full record for every case.

Inline actioning

Common responses — acknowledge, escalate, schedule follow-up — became inline actions instead of modal hops, cutting clicks per decision roughly in half.

Dual-coded risk, not color-only

Risk levels were encoded with both color and iconography/shape, so the system stayed usable for clinicians with color vision deficiencies — a non-negotiable in a clinical tool.

Outcomes

Faster triage, without adding clinical headcount.

Additional clinical modules built on this pattern

Follow-up surveys showed a marked drop in clinician-reported alert fatigue, and the severity-first triage pattern became the base pattern for two subsequent clinical modules — a system, not a one-off screen.

Reflection

What I'd tell another startup facing this.

In high-stakes workflows, the biggest UX win is rarely a new feature — it's re-ranking what the user sees first. Spend the research budget on watching real usage before touching the interface; the fix is often information architecture, not visual design.
