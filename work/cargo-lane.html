Whiteboard to shipped marketplace MVP in 8 weeks

Cargo Lane had a thesis — match independent truckers with shippers who had unpredictable freight — and two engineers. I designed a concierge-model MVP that validated demand on both sides before a line of matching-algorithm code was written.

Client
Cargo Lane (pre-seed marketplace)
Role
Founding Product Designer
Timeline
8 weeks
Team
2 founders · 2 engineers · me
Overview The problem Constraints Process Key decisions Outcomes Reflection

Overview

A thesis, not yet a product.

Cargo Lane's founders believed independent truckers were leaving capacity on the table because unpredictable, one-off freight jobs were hard to find through existing broker networks. It was a strong thesis with zero validation and a seed round riding on proving it fast. My job was to get to real usage evidence before their runway or their credibility ran out.

The problem

Two user types, opposing needs, no product yet.

Shippers needed confidence that a carrier would show up reliably for unpredictable, often short-notice freight. Carriers — frequently working from a truck cab, on unreliable connectivity, with limited comfort in software — needed something closer to a phone call than an app. Neither side would engage with a marketplace that felt empty, and neither would join first. This was a liquidity and trust problem before it was ever an interface problem.

Constraints

Pre-seed reality.

Engineering capacity

Two generalist engineers, no dedicated backend or mobile specialist — anything designed had to be buildable fast, by generalists.

Budget

Near-zero budget for research incentives or paid user recruitment — discovery had to run on founder and design-led outreach.

Carrier context

Low tech-literacy and inconsistent connectivity on the carrier side ruled out anything requiring a polished native app experience on day one.

Timeline

Eight weeks to real, defensible usage data ahead of seed-round conversations already in motion.

Process

Validate the thesis before building the engine.

  • Ran 15 discovery interviews across both sides of the marketplace in the first 10 days — 8 shippers, 7 independent carriers.
  • Mapped the trust and liquidity problem explicitly before sketching any interface — who needs to trust whom, and what would earn it.
  • Designed a "wizard-of-oz" matching model — the product looked automated, but early matches were made manually — to test real demand before investing engineering time in matching logic.
  • Designed carrier-first flows for low-literacy, low-bandwidth contexts, prioritizing SMS over app screens.
  • Shipped a lean shipper-facing web app paired with an SMS-based carrier flow, live within the eight-week window.

Key decisions

Concierge first, automation later.

SMS over native app for carriers

Chose SMS-based flows over a native app to remove install friction entirely and meet carriers on a channel they already trusted and used daily — while cutting weeks off the build.

Concierge-model matching

The matching "algorithm" was a human founder making calls behind the scenes in week one — the interface was designed to make this feel seamless to users while giving the team real signal on match quality before automating anything.

Trust-first shipper dashboard

Designed carrier reliability scores into the shipper-facing dashboard from day one, even with a small initial pool — surfacing trust signals early rather than waiting for scale to justify them.

Designed for evidence, not polish

Every screen was scoped to answer a specific question the founders needed for their seed deck — will shippers post unpredictable freight? Will carriers respond to SMS matches? — rather than to look finished.

Outcomes

Real usage data, not a roadmap guess.

46

Core evidence set used in their seed deck

The concierge MVP gave Cargo Lane's founders real usage data instead of a hypothesis — which directly shaped the roadmap for their first serious engineering investment: an actual matching engine, scoped against real behavior instead of guesses.

Reflection

What I'd tell another startup facing this.

At pre-seed, the highest-leverage design decision is usually what not to automate yet. Designing an interface that looks complete while the backend is still manual is a legitimate strategy — it buys you real evidence at a fraction of the engineering cost of building the real thing first.
